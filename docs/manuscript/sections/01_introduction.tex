\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pain is one of the most common reasons patients seek care in the emergency department (ED), and pain assessment is considered a core component of emergency care.
Clinical guidelines emphasize not only the initial evaluation of pain but also ongoing reassessment, as repeated pain assessments help clinicians evaluate disease progression, treatment response, and the need for additional interventions.
Despite its importance, pain reassessment remains a relatively understudied quality-of-care process compared with other ED performance metrics.

Prior studies examining pain care have largely focused on treatment outcomes such as analgesic administration, opioid prescribing, or disparities in pain management.
However, pain reassessment represents a distinct clinical process that reflects ongoing patient monitoring and clinical attention throughout the ED encounter.
Delays in reassessment may affect symptom recognition, treatment escalation, and overall patient experience.
Understanding which patients are reassessed more quickly---or more slowly---may therefore provide insight into how clinical attention is allocated within routine ED workflows.

Pain reassessment is likely influenced by multiple factors.
Patient presentation characteristics such as pain severity, diagnosis, and physiologic instability may appropriately drive more frequent reassessment.
At the same time, patient demographics, insurance status, language factors, triage acuity, arrival characteristics, and operational ED conditions may also influence reassessment timing.
These factors may interact through complex care pathways that shape how patients move through the ED and how clinical resources are allocated.

The objective of this study was to identify patient, clinical, and workflow factors associated with time to first pain reassessment in the emergency department.
Rather than focusing on a single demographic characteristic, we sought to characterize the broader set of factors that may influence reassessment timing, including clinical presentation, demographic and social factors, illness severity, and operational workflow characteristics.
Using a large electronic health record cohort, we evaluated associations under a sequential modeling framework designed to distinguish early-encounter patient and clinical factors from ED operational context.
